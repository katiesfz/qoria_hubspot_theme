KCSIE 2026 updates

Changes to Part one: Safeguarding information for all staff

Annex A, which previously provided a summary of Part One of KCSIE for staff who do not work directly with children, has been removed. According to the DfE, “All staff need a broader safeguarding awareness which Part one provides.”

Substantial changes

Child sexual exploitation

Research shows that victims of child exploitation are sometimes criminalised for actions taken under coercion. To ensure education professionals understand this dynamic and respond appropriately, paragraph 41 now states:

“Victims are not always recognised and can be criminalised for actions they take whilst under coercion... professionals continue to encounter cases where children are manipulated, groomed, and exploited without fully understanding the abuse they are experiencing.”

Violence between children

This section (paragraphs 29-34) is updated to highlight that this can be a safeguarding issue for both the victim and perpetrator of child-on-child violence.

This aligns with “a more trauma-informed approach to safeguarding.” The section also clarifies that violence may take the form of physical assault and threats with weapons.

Language updates

TopicUpdated guidance
Nude imagery & AI“Consensual and non-consensual sharing of nude and semi-nude images and/or videos (also known as sexting or youth-produced sexual imagery)” is replaced with: “Consensual and non-consensual self-generated intimate images and/or videos including those generated using AI e.g. deepfakes.”
MisogynyIncreased references (11 mentions, versus 3 in 2025) as this is increasingly prevalent. The guidance repeatedly highlights where misogyny intersects with harmful sexual behaviour, e.g., in the normalisation of sexual harassment.
Financial exploitationAppears in paragraph 20 in the list of risks staff need to consider if children are vulnerable to, and the section on modern slavery. Financial exploitation may take the form of fraud, scams, or financially-motivated sextortion.

Clarifications

  • To address a lack of awareness of the EYFS framework, this guidance is now referenced, applying to school nurseries and reception classes for children up to the age of 5.
  • Paragraphs 13 and 14 are updated to better align with the guidance from Working Together to Safeguard Children. This includes expanded instructions on multi-agency arrangements.
  • Paragraph 17 (Early Help) is amended to include: “has been repeatedly removed from the classroom” and “is on a part-time timetable”. This reflects Behaviour in Schools guidance and acknowledges important contributing factors to child criminal exploitation.
  • Wordings of paragraph 26 and 39 are updated to prevent confusion around the legal definition of rape in the UK, which is when a person uses their penis without consent to penetrate the vagina, anus, or mouth of another person.
  • Guidance around headteachers/principals considering referrals to the LADO in response to safeguarding concerns or allegations about other members of staff appears earlier in the document - in paragraph 75.

Changes to Part two: The management of safeguarding

The Department for Education will not publish standalone guidance for schools and colleges on gender questioning children, and will instead incorporate this guidance into KCSIE.

Mental health

Significant revisions have been made to the Children requiring mental health support section. The updates focus on how mental health concerns can intersect with safeguarding duties, particularly around risks including self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal ideation. While staff are not expected to diagnose or treat mental health issues, the guidance clarifies the important role they play in both mitigating and identifying safeguarding risks linked to mental health.

Gender questioning children

The guidance clarifies that it is not the role of schools and colleges to initiate action in this area, but rather they should take a cautious, understanding approach and adopt a flexible stance on gender stereotypes.

Two new sections cover single-sex spaces and single-sex sports, aligning with the Equality Act 2010 and Human Rights Act 1998.

  • Paragraphs 104-115 explain that when it comes to toilets, showers and changing facilities, children must not be allowed into the spaces “designated for the opposite biological sex.” In instances where a child does not want to use these spaces, “schools and colleges should consider whether they can provide an alternative.”
  • Paragraphs 94-97 confirm that schools and colleges can lawfully separate children according to their biological sex for sport. In cases where a gender questioning child makes a request relating to how they participate, if “there are no safety concerns”, settings should consider “whether supporting social transition is overall in the best interests of the child, as well as considering the impact on other children and the aim of creating safe and fair environments for children to participate in PE.”

AI

The Online safety section is expanded to include two new paragraphs to help schools and colleges “understand safety considerations and legal responsibilities if they choose to use Generative AI.”

Paragraphs 160-161 highlight the policy paper, Generative artificial intelligence in education, and online resources produced by the Chiltern Learning Trust and Chartered College of Teaching, which can support schools and colleges to use AI safely.

Mobile phone policy

The Online safety section also includes a new paragraph (163) on mobile phone policy, confirming that “all schools should be mobile phone-free environments by default.” It provides links to two resources, Mobile phones in schools and Creating a mobile phone-free environment: school case studies, which can support schools in developing a policy suitable for their setting.

Filtering & monitoring

“Reviews should include checks that filtering is working appropriately on all internet-connected devices in all relevant locations, and a record should be kept of these checks.”

The filtering and monitoring guidance is updated to match wording in the Filtering and Monitoring Standards. Schools and colleges are recommended to take a more proactive approach to these systems, “particularly given the increasing risks from harmful digital content, AI generated material, and rapid changes in online behaviour.”

Language updates & clarifications

Requirements for DSL role

Paragraph 125 highlights the need for governing bodies and proprietors to ensure that designated safeguarding leads have the appropriate “skills and experience” to be able to fulfil their duties, alongside the existing requisite of “status” and "authority."

Preventative education

Paragraph 153 expands the description of zero tolerance culture to include “derogatory behaviour or other forms of physical violence and conflict.”

Programmes are expected to address “online harms such as sharing images, the prevalence of deepfakes, pornography and misogynistic influencers and when and where to seek help.”

Cyber security

The section on Information security and access management now recognises cyber security as a safeguarding concern. If a child’s sensitive data is compromised by a cyber attack, it poses a risk to their safety and wellbeing.

Paragraphs 170-172 emphasise the need for schools to strengthen their cyber security systems to “reduce the risk of disruption, prevent data breaches, and mitigate safeguarding risks.”

Alternative provision

The Alternative provision section references the National standards for non-school alternative provision, which may be used by local authorities to develop a register of approved settings for schools to use.

This is intended to “strengthen safeguarding oversight for children educated outside traditional school settings.”

Medical conditions

Paragraph 243 explains that while medical conditions do not necessarily indicate that a child is at greater safeguarding risk, in the event that clinical incidents occur at a school or college, healthcare professionals should work with DSLs to consider whether a safeguarding duty is triggered.

SEND or health issues

The list of additional barriers that exist when recognising abuse, neglect or exploitation in children with SEND or health issues has been expanded.

Paragraph 240 now includes “the need for intimate care or that these children are isolated from others”, “that these children are more likely to be dependent on adults for care”, and clarifies that communication difficulties can mean that SEND children may not understand that what is happening to them is abuse.

Information sharing

The Information sharing section explains that, in cases where children move settings, the DSL may consider sharing information beyond the child protection file if it indicates that the student may be a risk to themselves or others.

It also states that it would be “good practice” for DSLs at both settings to have a conversation about potential issues or concerns.

Changes to Part three: Safer recruitment

Adults who supervise children on work experience

The language around Disclosure and Barring Service (DBS) checks and regulated activity has been tweaked. This is in response to sector feedback on unnecessary DBS checks being requested, and also to reflect policy changes on work experience.

For example, “employer” is replaced with “placement provider” or “work experience provider”. The decision-making criteria for DBS checks is tightened from “the specific circumstances of the work experience” to two “relevant considerations”: whether the provider is unsupervised, and the frequency of the training.

Single Central Record

Page 93 includes an example of a single central record that meets statutory requirements.

Changes to Part four: Safeguarding concerns or allegations made about staff, including supply teachers, volunteers and contractors

Allegations against trainee teachers

“Trainee teacher” is now included throughout this section, to clarify that people in this position are subject to the same procedures, should an allegation be made against them. A new paragraph (450) also confirms that teacher training providers should follow the same procedures set out for supply agencies.

Changes to Part five: Child-on-child sexual harassment and sexual violence

This section is restructured to “present a clearer and more progressive continuum of sexual behaviours.” It now starts with early indicators of harmful sexual behaviour and ends with sexual violence, for a more logical flow.

Paragraph 521, which lists harmful sexual behaviours that schools and colleges should be aware of, is updated to include “recognising the escalatory nature of misogyny and the benefits of early identification to support their approach to minimising the risk of HSB, sexual harassment and sexual violence.”

Changes to Annex A (previously Annex B): Further information

The removal of Annex A, means what was Annex B (which provides further detailed guidance on a range of safeguarding issues) is now Annex A.

Cybercrime

The Cybercrime section is expanded to make schools and colleges aware that this issue “continues to represent one of the most significant and rapidly evolving threats within the United Kingdom’s (UK) crime landscape.” Young people partake in such activity, “often without understanding the legal or ethical implications” and the impact “extends well beyond immediate financial losses, with scope to cause disruption to national infrastructure.”

Online safety advice

The Online safety section includes two new links to address emerging digital risks:

  • Understanding and responding to AI-generated child sexual abuse material
  • Financially motivated sexual extortion (FMSE)

Changes to Annex B (previously Annex C): The role of the designated safeguarding lead

Robust cover arrangements

To ensure safeguarding concerns can be addressed without delay, guidelines on the importance of having robust cover arrangements for DSLs have been added. For example, it suggests that cover arrangements could include “a confidential shared mailbox or equivalent system” to ensure issues can be dealt with immediately.

The 4 key roles for staff

4 key roles for staff are outlined:

  1. Promoting good mental wellbeing and preventing the onset of mental illness
  2. Observing pupils and identifying early those who may be experiencing mental health problems or being at risk of developing one
  3. Ensuring early targeted support is provided
  4. Liaison with/referral to specialist services where needed
